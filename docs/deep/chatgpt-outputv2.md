## How the premarket fill filter should be wired

The cleanest place to wire the pre-market filter is after the existing overnight gap-size check and before the first-bar rejection logic branches into long-versus-short entry construction. In plain English: day qualifies on cash gap size first, then qualifies on pre-market structure, then qualifies on first-bar rejection, then proceeds to stop/target construction and order placement. That preserves the current strategy definition instead of mixing a regime filter into the bar-confirmation rule or into execution. It also means failed pre-market days can be logged as a structural skip reason rather than as a missed signal or an execution failure. That placement matches the current `run_gap_fade()` structure, where size bounds are checked first and directional entry logic is only considered after that. fileciteturn0file3

## What threshold is actually defensible

The evidence-first answer is not the exciting one. If your internal bins are `<30%`, `30–70%`, and `>=70%`, the most defensible live rule today is to reject only the clearly toxic left tail and leave the rest alone. In other words, the conservative pre-registered filter is `skip if premarket_fill_pct < 0.30`, not `trade only if premarket_fill_pct >= 0.70`. The reason is simple: your internal sample, as you described it, shows a clean sign change between the worst bucket and the two non-awful buckets. Moving from “remove the obviously bad regime” to “optimize for the best sub-bucket” is where overfitting risk rises sharply, especially on 57 trades. Inference: a 30% floor is a robustness filter; a 70% floor is already an alpha optimization. fileciteturn0file9

## What out of sample validation should look like

A defensible validation plan has four parts. First, freeze one binary rule only. Do not test 30%, 40%, 50%, 60%, and 70% in live paper after already seeing the retrospective bins. Second, carry the exact same fill-definition from research into production. Third, evaluate chronologically, not by random reshuffle. Fourth, keep the filter decision separate from the strategy decision in the logs so you can later ask whether the filter genuinely improved trade quality or merely reduced trade count. General walk-forward and out-of-sample research keeps converging on the same point: robustness comes from stable regions and chronological forward testing, not from the single best fitted cutoff. citeturn27academia1turn27academia2turn27academia3

For your project specifically, I would separate “validation to turn the filter on” from “validation to declare the strategy proven.” Gap fade is still on hold pending short-path verification and then a minimum live paper sample before drawing conclusions. So the clean implementation is to deploy the filter in shadow first for every eligible day, logging both `would_trade_under_filter` and `actual_trade_under_current_rule`, until you accumulate enough fresh forward cases to compare the toxic bucket against the non-toxic bucket without further tuning. Because your own pre-registered process already insists on waiting for meaningful live counts before strategy conclusions, shadow-testing the filter is fully aligned with the project’s governance style. fileciteturn0file7 fileciteturn0file9

## What is structurally different about ORB shorts

The first asymmetry is regulatory and broker-operational, not statistical. In real trading, short sales sit inside Regulation SHO. SEC guidance says a broker-dealer must have reasonable grounds to believe the security can be borrowed before effecting a short sale, and must document that locate. Rule 201 then adds a short-sale price test once a stock is down at least 10% on the day, blocking short sales at an impermissible price for the rest of that day and the next one. Those frictions do not exist for long entries, and your simulated environment will not fully model them. So paper trading can validate logic, but not the final live feasibility of short execution. citeturn17view0turn17view1turn16view0

The second asymmetry is market-microstructure shape. Early in the day, volatility is highest and trading is more idiosyncratic; the market factor becomes more dominant later. Separate high-frequency work on SPY also finds that large negative pushes are followed by stronger positive responses than equally large positive pushes, which the authors interpret as asymmetric liquidity replenishment after sell-side shocks. In practical terms, that means a downside breakout can be “right” for a moment and still be followed by a more violent reflex bounce than an upside breakout would typically face. That is exactly the kind of path asymmetry that can make short ORB fills look worse even if the raw signal logic is symmetric on paper. citeturn31view4turn31view5turn31view0

The third asymmetry is cross-sectional. Newer market-cap research finds that lower-market-cap stocks show more order modifications and less order inertia in the opening period than higher-cap names. BlackRock’s own IWM product materials also warn that small-capitalization companies are less stable, more volatile, and less liquid than larger-cap names. Since IWM is your one symbol with a 30-minute OR window and small-cap exposure, the short side there deserves extra suspicion: not because “small caps go up or down more,” but because their opening microstructure can be choppier and less stable. That does not prove IWM shorts are bad. It does mean the first place I would expect asymmetric slippage, fake breakdowns, and fast stop-outs is IWM, not SPY or QQQ. citeturn31view1turn31view2turn25view1

The fourth asymmetry is squeeze mechanics. The SEC’s own investor guidance notes that short squeezes arise when short sellers are forced to cover because of rising prices or difficulty borrowing the security, and that this covering can itself add upward pressure. For SPY and QQQ, borrow difficulty is usually a smaller issue than in single names because they are extremely liquid ETFs; State Street reports SPY with roughly $787 billion in AUM as of June 16, 2026, and Invesco describes QQQ as the second-most traded ETF in the U.S. by average daily volume as of March 31, 2026. So your production concern is less “hard-to-borrow catastrophe” and more “fast air-pocket down move followed by violent buyback and cover cascade.” That is still a real asymmetry versus longs, just a milder one than you would face shorting individual stocks. citeturn32view0turn23view0turn24view2

## What to watch in the first weeks of live short data

Do not judge the short side first by PnL. Judge it first by symmetry of machinery. You want to know whether `SELL_SHORT` entries, stop logic, target logic, and `BUY_TO_COVER` exits behave exactly like the long side under the same logging and confirmation rules. Your project already learned the hard way that apparent paper PnL can be fictional when orders do not actually fill, and ORB is already the strategy where execution quality matters most. So the first dashboard for shorts should compare entry fill rate, median entry slippage versus intended price, median time-to-fill, percent of fills that reverse to stop within the next two bars, exit fill quality on `BUY_TO_COVER`, and reject/cancel/retry behavior relative to long ORB. fileciteturn0file5 fileciteturn0file6 fileciteturn0file9
